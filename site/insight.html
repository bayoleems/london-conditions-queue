Approval of details

Permission granted is not permission to build. More than half of London's condition sign-offs miss the 8-week deadline.

Most planning permissions come with conditions: drainage, construction logistics, materials, cycle stores. Many must be signed off before work can start. We analysed 17,079 applications to discharge conditions on London housing schemes, submitted 2021–2025.

1 · The clock

Councils work to the deadline, and a long tail sits behind it

Councils have eight weeks to decide an application to approve details required by a condition (Development Management Procedure Order 2015, article 27). 22% of all decisions land in the final week before that deadline. After it passes, nothing forces a decision, and the queue thins out slowly over months.

2 · Getting better, slowly

The late share has fallen since 2021, but it has stalled at about half

Applications submitted in 2021 came out of a pandemic backlog. Since 2024 the share decided late has sat around half.

3 · Bundling

Applications that bundle many conditions are the slowest and most often refused

One application can ask for several conditions to be signed off at once. With seven or more, 85% are decided late, the median wait is 104 days, and 29% are refused or only partly approved. A single refused item sends the whole package back.

4 · What gets stuck

Drainage and construction logistics are both slow and often refused

Drainage plans and construction logistics plans are usually needed before work can start. Both run late more than 60% of the time, and about one in five is refused. Fire safety details are refused at a similar rate.

5 · Over a scheme's life

Major schemes come back for condition sign-offs about seven times on average

We matched discharge applications to 1,460 permissions granted 2021–2023, giving each at least two and a half years to come back. Developers take their own time too: the median first submission comes 181 days after permission. When a discharge is refused and resubmitted, the approval comes a median 69 days after the refusal (378 matched pairs).

6 · Boroughs

Practice varies more than speed

The share decided late runs from about 40% in Enfield to 71% in Bexley. Refusal practice varies far more: Camden and Wandsworth almost never refuse a discharge, while Croydon refuses or part-refuses over a third. A refusal is fast for the council but costs the applicant another round.

What could change

Four levers the data points to

Make deemed discharge real

Since 2015, applicants can serve notice that many conditions are treated as approved once the deadline passes. The extract records only 16 deemed discharges against about 8,300 late decisions, 13 of them in Croydon. Only 3 of 28 boroughs record any, so the route is either almost unused or invisible in most council systems. Either way, nobody can tell how often applicants use it. Recording it consistently would be the first step.

Standard London conditions

Construction logistics, drainage and cycle/refuse storage come up thousands of times. London-wide standard wording and templates would cut both refusals and back-and-forth.

Fewer pre-start conditions

Applications bundling seven or more conditions are late 85% of the time. Settling drainage and logistics detail at the application stage for larger schemes would shorten the queue after permission.

Publish the clock

Discharge decisions get far less scrutiny than the headline speed figures for planning applications. Boroughs already hold these dates, so a quarterly table would cost little.

Method and caveats
  • Data: PlanIt-derived extract of London planning applications (foundations_london_housing_2015_2025, 243,926 rows, in practice 2021–2025). Fetched August 2026, so applications submitted up to December 2025 had at least seven months to be decided.
  • What counts as a discharge application: descriptions mentioning approval or discharge of details under a condition, excluding variations (section 73), non-material amendments and reserved matters, and limited to those that mention homes. 17,079 applications; 16,447 submitted 2021–2025 and not withdrawn.
  • Late means more than 56 days from the start date to the decision. This includes extensions of time agreed with the applicant, so late is not the same as unlawful. Counting decided applications only gives 53.8%; adding undecided applications over 8 weeks old gives 55.2%.
  • Extensions of time have no field of their own in the extract. 76% of decided applications carry a council target decision date, and measuring those against their own target gives 52.5% late, against 53.0% at a fixed 8 weeks. Only 5.3% were late at 8 weeks but on time against a later target, most of them in Hillingdon. Agreed extensions therefore explain a few points of the late share at most. Target dates are not used consistently: Camden, Merton and Enfield often set targets shorter than 56 days, and nine boroughs, including Brent and Wandsworth, record none.
  • Coverage is uneven. Some boroughs' discharge applications are mostly missing from the extract. Borough comparisons use only the 15 boroughs with at least 300 applications.
  • Topics, condition counts and scheme size come from keyword and pattern matching on free-text descriptions. Scheme size is unknown for about half of applications.
  • Scheme matching: 5,664 discharge applications (33%) were matched to their parent permission by reference number. Many parents were granted before 2021 and are outside the extract.
  • Deemed discharge is counted from decision labels containing “deemed” together with “discharge” or “AOD”. Only Croydon (13), Southwark (2) and Islington (1) record any. Other boroughs may log a deemed discharge as an ordinary approval, so 16 is a floor, not a London total.
  • Reproduce: python -m conditions_queue.analysis writes outputs/summary.json, outputs/discharge_apps.csv and outputs/schemes.csv. conditions_queue.checks.check_deadline and check_extensions re-derive the deadline, deemed discharge and extension figures independently.
